FN-105 introduced the three-way split and DS-201 the idea of an interval. This session adds the question FN-105 left open: which rows go on which side, and why.

The principle

A validation split is a rehearsal of how the model will be used

Who is new in use?

New rows of known people, or people the model has never seen? If people are new, they must be new in validation.

What is later in use?

The model is trained once and scores what comes afterwards. Then validation rows come after training rows.

What is known on that day?

An outcome that does not exist yet cannot train a model. Training rows are those whose outcome was known.

The split is chosen from the use, before any score is seen.

For Mubakkir: each January and July a model is trained on what the fund knows and scores the intake of the next half-year. Most trainees are new; some return. The fund's folds rehearse exactly that. Sources: scikit-learn user guide, Cross-validation.

Three ways to cut the same rows

Random, grouped and time-ordered splits answer different questions

SplitHow it cutsThe question it answers
RandomEach row lands in a fold by chanceHow well does the model do on more rows like these, from the same period?
GroupedAll rows of one trainee stay on one sideHow well does it do on a trainee it has never seen?
Time-orderedTrain on the past, validate on a later half-yearHow well does it do on the next intake?
None is wrong in itself. A split is wrong for a use. A random split is right when rows are independent and the model is used on rows like them.

In scikit-learn: StratifiedKFold, GroupKFold and TimeSeriesSplit, or folds you build yourself as the fund does. Participants write the first two in Lab 3.1.

A detail that decides the folds

Train on programmes that were due to end, not on those that had ended

  • On 1 July 2024 a 36-week programme that began in March is still running
  • Its early leavers already have an outcome; its other trainees have none
  • "Every row with a known outcome" keeps the leavers and drops their classmates
  • The model then learns that recent long programmes always fail

The fund's rule

A row trains a fold only when its programme's planned end lies before the validation half-year begins. The whole class enters together, or not at all.

This is label maturity: a label needs time to exist, and it arrives at different speeds for the two classes. The trainer's first version of these folds used the exit date and produced a model that was far worse on recent cohorts for no visible reason. It is the most common fault in time-ordered validation of outcomes that take months to settle: churn, default, readmission.

Beyond this table

When does a random split overstate badly?

Many rows per unit

Thousands of readings per machine, dozens of visits per patient. The model recognises the unit. Here: at most four rows per trainee.

Features built from other rows

A rate or a count that read the validation rows or the future. The next three sessions.

A world that moves fast

Prices, fraud patterns, policies. The faster the change, the more a model's age costs.

The habit. Choose the split from the use. Then measure the other splits once, and write the gaps down with their causes.

Leakage is information in training that will not be there in use. This session names four kinds and gives each a question; the lab applies them to six cases from other sectors.

A taxonomy

Leakage comes in four kinds

Target leakage

A column written because of the outcome, or together with it.

Future information

A value computed from records later than the prediction time.

Group or duplicate contamination

The same person, machine or row on both sides of the split.

Preparation leakage

A step fitted or chosen on rows that later score the model.

All four give a score in validation that the model cannot repeat in use.

The first two are faults of a column, the third of the split, the fourth of the procedure. Real cases often show two at once; the taxonomy tells you where to look. Sources: Kapoor and Narayanan (2023), who found leakage in published work across 17 fields.

Kind 1

Target leakage: the column knows the answer

  • Recorded when the outcome happens: exit_reason, weeks_attended
  • A consequence of the outcome: calls from the collections team after a missed payment
  • Someone's answer to the same question: an antibiotic ordered because the doctor suspects the infection

The question

When is this column written, and by whom? Would a person who already knew the outcome be the one filling it in?

What goes wrong. The model looks brilliant and the top feature is one nobody questioned because it "obviously matters".

Day 1 already removed three such columns with the data dictionary. The hard cases are the indirect ones, like the antibiotic: the column is recorded before the outcome is confirmed, yet it exists only because a human has half-made the prediction.

Kind 2

Future information: right column, wrong date

  • A history that counts later records: all non-completions of a trainee, whenever they happened
  • A yearly average attached to a row from March
  • A status column overwritten in place: today's value on an old row

The question

On the day of the prediction, what would the value of this feature have been? Rebuild it with later records hidden and compare.

The time-travel test of Lab 1.4 is the instrument for this kind. The feature is legitimate in idea, a history, and wrong in construction. Overwritten columns are the quiet version: a database that keeps only the current employment status puts today's status on a row from 2022.

Kind 3

Group or duplicate contamination: the model has met the test rows

  • Many rows of one unit, split at random: hours of one pump, visits of one patient
  • The same record under two keys after two systems were merged
  • Copies made on purpose: classes "balanced" by duplicating rows before the split

The question

What is the unit the model meets in use? Can the same real-world event appear in the table more than once?

A unique key proves nothing about unique rows. Look for rows that agree on everything that describes the event. The third bullet comes back this afternoon in Loay's notebook.

Kind 4

Preparation leakage: something was fitted before the split

  • Scaling or imputing with statistics of all rows
  • Encoding a category by its outcome rate over all rows
  • Selecting the "best" features with the outcome of all rows
  • Choosing settings on the rows that report the score

The question

Was any step fitted or chosen before the split? Everything that learns from data belongs inside the pipeline, fitted on training rows only.

The first bullet usually costs little, as Lab 1.3 measured; the third can turn noise into an accuracy of 0.9 when there are hundreds of candidate columns. Tuning on the test rows is the same fault, which is why Day 2 nested its search. Sources: scikit-learn user guide, Common pitfalls and recommended practices.

A judgment

The size of a leak is measured; whether it is a leak is decided by how the column was made

  • provider_rate moved the score by 0.002
  • Each provider has several hundred rows: one row's own outcome hardly changes the rate
  • With five rows per provider the same column would dominate
  • And it read the 2025 cohort, the holdout

Why remove a small leak

The measured effect belongs to this table and this model. The fault belongs to the procedure, and the procedure will be reused on the next table.

Before any code

Three questions to any reported score

On which rows?

How many test rows, from which period, with what share of the outcome? Do they look like the population the model will score?

Against what?

What does a constant answer score on the same rows? What did the previous model score?

Chosen how?

How many versions were tried, and on which rows was this one chosen?

"95% accuracy" answers none of them.

Loay's own output already answers the first two against him: 15,017 test rows when a fifth of 45,000 would be 9,000, and half of them non-completers when the fund's rate is one in six. Reading the printed numbers beside the headline is a review before the review.

This is the order of the data's journey, not of the notebook's cells: a notebook often prepares before it splits, and that is already a finding. The order is the same one the capstone assessor will use on each repository.

The fault that did the damage

"Balancing the classes" before the split copies test rows into training

  • Each non-completer is copied four times, then the table is split at random
  • Most test non-completers have a copy in training
  • A tree with five rows per leaf recognises them
  • The test set is half non-completers: no cohort looks like it

If you balance at all

Inside the training part only, as a step of the pipeline. The test rows keep the real share. Tomorrow shows that a ranking does not need it.

From Lab 3.4, block 2. The same holds for synthetic oversampling: generated neighbours of a test row are near copies of it. This single block accounts for almost the whole of the 95%.

What you send back

A review note names numbers, not character
