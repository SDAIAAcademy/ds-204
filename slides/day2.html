Many small trees

Boosting adds one small tree at a time, each correcting the sum so far

  • Start from the base rate
  • Fit a small tree to what the current model still gets wrong
  • Add a fraction of it: the learning rate
  • Repeat a few hundred times
  • The prediction is the sum of all the trees

Why it leads on tables

It handles mixed columns, missing values and interactions with little preparation, and it runs on a laptop processor. On medium-sized tables, tuned tree ensembles have matched or beaten deep networks in published comparisons.

LightGBM, XGBoost and CatBoost are three implementations of the same idea; the labs use LightGBM because it is fast on a processor. Sources: Grinsztajn, Oyallon and Varoquaux (2022), a benchmark on 45 tabular datasets of medium size.

A boosted model scores 0.85 on its 9,068 training rows and 0.50 on the validation rows of the same fold. What do you conclude?

The idea that carries the session: a score that was used to choose something is no longer a clean estimate. Nested folds are the answer, and a stated budget is its companion.

What to tune

A few settings trade flexibility against stability

How much is learnt

The number of trees and the learning rate. They work as a pair: a smaller rate needs more trees.

How complex each tree is

Leaves per tree, and the fewest rows a leaf may hold. Fewer leaves and larger leaves: less to memorise.

How much is held back

Each tree sees a share of the rows and of the inputs; a penalty shrinks the leaf values.

Lab 2.2 searches seven settings with 2,592 combinations. The budget is 20 of them.

The search space is in the notebook: 100 to 400 trees, a rate of 0.02 to 0.1, 4 to 31 leaves, 20 to 200 rows per leaf, two sampling shares and a penalty. Sources: LightGBM documentation, Parameters tuning.

The rule

State the budget before the search, and draw the trials at random

  • A budget: 20 trials, fixed in advance, with a seed
  • Random search: each trial draws every setting independently
  • With a few settings that matter and several that hardly do, random draws cover the important ones better than a grid
  • Every trial is kept, the poor ones too

Why a budget is about honesty

Each trial is another look at the validation rows. A search that stops "when the score looks good" has no defined number of looks, and its best score cannot be interpreted.

Smarter searches exist (Bayesian optimisation, successive halving) and automated tools use them. They change how the budget is spent, not the need for one. Sources: Bergstra and Bengio (2012).

Nested validation

Inner folds choose, the outer fold reports

1 · Outer foldSet its validation half-year aside
→
2 · Inner foldsInside the training part, score 20 trials on two later half-years
→
3 · RefitThe best settings, on the whole training part
→
4 · ReportOne score on the half-year no trial has seen
  • Repeated for each of the five outer folds: five searches, five scores
  • What is validated is the procedure "search 20 trials, then fit"
  • The inner folds are built like the outer ones: later half-years

This costs five searches in place of one: 140 seconds on a laptop for this table. The inner score and the outer score sit on different rows, so they are not compared as numbers: one chooses, the other reports. Sources: scikit-learn example, Nested versus non-nested cross-validation.

The champion needs an opponent it did not choose itself. Two kinds exist today: tools that automate what you did in Lab 2.2, and models pretrained on other tables.

Two kinds of challenger

One automates the search; the other arrives already trained

Automated machine learning

You give the training rows, a metric and a time limit. The tool prepares the columns, trains several model families, tunes them and blends the best. Example: AutoGluon Tabular. It trains on your machine, on your rows only.

A tabular foundation model

A network pretrained on millions of synthetic tables. It reads your training rows as context and predicts the new rows in one pass, with no tuning. Example: TabPFN. Published results are strongest on small tables.

Both are challengers, not shortcuts. Each has to stand on the same folds as the champion, in the same log.

The lab runs the first kind. The second kind is discussed and not run: its weights are a download of a pretrained model, which the labs of this course avoid, and its licence needs the reading of the next slide. Sources: AutoGluon documentation, Tabular essentials; Hollmann and others (2025).

The key idea

Two models on the same folds give pairs, and pairs are compared by their differences

An interval on the mean difference

Five differences give an honest, wide interval

−0.007
mean difference, challenger minus champion; 95% interval −0.018 to +0.004
  • Mean ± t × (spread of the differences ÷ √5)
  • With 5 folds the multiplier is 2.78, not 1.96
  • The interval contains zero: no difference is shown
  • The folds share training rows, so the true uncertainty is, if anything, larger

From Lab 2.4: differences −0.009, −0.021, +0.001, +0.001, −0.008. The t interval treats the five folds as a sample of the half-years the model might meet. It is the simplest defensible interval and the one the course outcome asks for.

A second opinion

The paired bootstrap resamples validation rows, not folds

  • Both models scored the same 21,770 validation rows
  • Draw rows again with replacement, inside each fold
  • Score both models on the same draw

From Lab 2.4. Here they differ, mildly: the bootstrap is narrower and excludes zero, the t interval does not. The decision rule

State the smallest gain worth having before you look

Adopt the challenger

The whole interval lies above the minimum effect.

Keep the champion

The whole interval lies below the minimum effect.

Not shown

For the fund: +0.01 average precision, agreed with Dr. Haya Al-Mogren before the contest.

The minimum effect is a business judgment, not a statistical one: a blend of seven models costs more to explain, to serve and to maintain than one model, and a gain below 0.01 changes almost nobody's place on a list of 800. Stated after the result, a minimum effect tends to land just below whatever was measured.
