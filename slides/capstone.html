A modest score that is validated beats a high one that is not. No criterion rewards the size of the number.

Full marks, one example each. Framing: a decision with an owner and a capacity, and a rule baseline on the same folds. Validation: the split argued from the use, other splits measured once, an audit table. Models: a stated budget and a paired interval. Threshold: a rule tested on rows opened once, a decile table, slices with intervals and a bias type per finding. Card: limits a reader can recognise, and a clone that runs.

The defence

Eight minutes: four are yours, four belong to one change

Minutes 0 to 4 · You present

The decision. The validation scheme and why. The ladder in one table. The finding you are least comfortable with.

Minutes 4 to 8 · One change

The assessor swaps the split or removes a feature. You say what will happen to the score, and why, before it is run. Then it is run, or the result is shown.

One timer, no separate question time. The change and your prediction are the questions.

The catalog gives eight minutes and no question time, so the change sits inside the eight. A prediction that turns out wrong and is then explained well earns more than a lucky one: the criterion is the reasoning about the validation choice.

What the assessor may ask

Predict the effect before it is run

The changeWhat a good answer contains
"Score it under a random split."The direction, a rough size, and which rows the training part would then know
"Keep each unit on one side."Whether the model can recognise a unit, and what history is worth
"Remove your strongest feature."What the feature carries, and which others can stand in for it
"Put back the column you removed."The kind of leak, and why the higher score cannot be repeated in use

Choose the change from the README: the weakest argued choice is the best one to ask about. When re-running takes longer than a minute, ask participants to have the alternative split ready as a switch, as Loay's notebook had, or show the result you prepared.
